\begin{abstract}
Linear recurrent neural networks (LRNNs) provide a simple yet powerful framework
for modeling time series. Unlike traditional recurrent neural networks, LRNNs
use linear activation functions and can be trained without backpropagation by
solving linear equation systems. This paper summarizes their theoretical
properties, learning procedure, and ability to approximate arbitrary
time-dependent functions. A key contribution is a method for reducing network
size using eigenvalue analysis, enabling both efficient learning and
architecture optimization, which is nowadays an important topic. We
demonstrate the properties and advantages of LRNNs by several case studies,
among them multiple superimposed oscillators (MSO), robotic soccer (RoboCup),
and stock price prediction. LRNNs outperform the previous state-of-the-art for
the MSO task with a minimal number of units.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Time-series modeling is essential in many domains such as forecasting, signal
processing, and control systems. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs), especially
LSTMs, are widely used but often require complex training procedures like
backpropagation through time. Research in time-series analysis and hence the
modeling dynamics of complex systems has a long tradition and remains highly
active due to its crucial role in many real-world applications \cite{LBE15}. Among
other benefits, the analysis of time series enables data compression, i.e., the
compact representation of time series by a function $f(t)$, and prediction of
future values.

Numerous studies address these topics using RNNs, particularly variants of
networks with long short-term memory (LSTM) \cite{HS97}. Autoregressive
frameworks \cite{Aka69} are also common in current machine-learning applications
such as large language models (LLMs). Nevertheless, usually iterative methods
like backpropagation are employed for neural network learning. This is not
needed for \emph{linear recurrent neural networks} (LRNNs), i.e., the class of
RNNs with linear activation, which we discuss here. Despite their simplicity,
LRNNs can approximate arbitrary time-dependent functions $f(t)$, can be trained
efficiently using simple linear algebra, and allow direct architecture reduction
via eigenvalue analysis.

\section{Linear Recurrent Neural Networks}

An LRNN consists of neurons and connections between them. They are annotated
with \emph{weights} which are compiled into the \emph{transition matrix} $W$ of
size $N \times N$, containing input, output and reservoir weight sub-matrices.
An entry~$w_{ij}$ in row~$i$ and column~$j$ denotes the weight of the edge from
neuron~$j$ to neuron~$i$. The network evolves as $f(t) = W^t s$ where $s$ is the
start state. Key properties of LRNNs are (1)~linear activation, (2)~random input
and reservoir weights, (3)~only output weights are learned, and (4)~the spectral
radius of the reservoir is normalized to $1$.

Using eigendecomposition $W = V D\,V^{-1}$ with column eigenvectors $v_1,\dots,v_N$
in $V$ and corresponding eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_N$, the system
evolves as $f(t) = x_1 \lambda_1^t v_1 +\dots+ x_N \lambda_N^t v_N$ with $x =
\big[ x_1 \cdots x_N \big]^\top$ such that $x = V^{-1}s$. This can be
generalized to Jordan decomposition with Jordan matrix $J$ and to arbitrary
start vectors $y$ with all nonzero entries. It can be shown that always a matrix
$A$ exists such that $f(t) = A J^t y$.

This shows that network behavior is governed by eigenvalues $\lambda$, in
particular the one with maximal absolute value (defining the spectral radius).
We have (1)~contraction to a singularity for $|\lambda| < 1$, (2)~oscillation
for $|\lambda| = 1$, and (3)~exponential growth for $|\lambda| > 1$. For large
$t$, LRNN dynamics simplify drastically: Most components vanish, the remaining
dynamics lie in at most $2$ dimensions, and the output follows an ellipse
trajectory. Thus, complex high-dimensional systems reduce to simple oscillatory
behavior over time.

\section{Learning Procedure and Network Size Reduction}

Training is performed via linear regression instead of backpropagation. Given
\[
X =
\begin{bmatrix}
S(0) & \cdots & S(n-1)\\
R(0) & \cdots & R(n-1)
\end{bmatrix}
\quad\text{and}\quad
Y =
\begin{bmatrix}
S(1)~& \cdots & S(n)
\end{bmatrix},
\]
where $S$ and $R$ are the possibly multi-dimensional input and reservoir time
series, we simply solve $W_{\text{out}} = Y X^{-1}$ to compute the output
weights. This is efficient and avoids gradient-based optimization. LRNNs
satisfy an approximation theorem: \emph{Any time-dependent function $f(t)$ can
be approximated arbitrarily well and learned given sufficient reservoir size.}

A major contribution of this paper is reducing network size using eigenvalue
analysis. The method works follows: After training, we (1)~compute the
eigenvalues of $W$, (2)~rank the Jordan components of $W$ by importance,
(3)~remove those with minimal contribution, and (4)~ensure that the error
remains below some threshold. This yields significantly smaller networks with
sparse structure, improved generalization of the time series, and faster
computation. Unlike pruning methods, network size reduction is performed in a
single step here.

\section{Properties, Insights, and Experimental Findings}

The learning procedure, even with network size reduction, is computationally
efficient in $O(N^3)$. No iterative optimization is required. Therefore, it is
faster than traditional RNN training. LRNNs are related to autoregressive models
\cite{Aka69} like current large language models (LLMs), echo state networks
(ESNs) \cite{JH04}, and state-space models \cite{GGR22,OS+23}. However, LRNNs
uniquely combine linear learning, exact approximation, and architecture
reduction.

Experiments demonstrate that LRNNs (1)~achieve high accuracy on time-series
prediction, (2)~outperform traditional methods on oscillatory tasks, and
(3)~learn compact representations, e.g., minimal networks. The following benchmarks
have been considered: multiple superimposed oscillators (MSO) \cite{KLB12},
number puzzles \cite{GW13}, robot soccer simulation \cite{MO+18}, and predicting
stock prices~\cite{Lit20}.

For instance, the MSO time series is generated by summing up $K$ (pure)
sinusoids. It requires only $2K$ neurons in LRNNs, where each frequency
corresponds to one eigenvalue pair. LRNNs outperform the previous
state of the art for the MSO task with this minimal number of units. All
experiments are performed with a program written by the authors in Octave
\cite{EB+17}, available at \url{http://github.com/OliverObst/decorating/}.

\section{Conclusion}

LRNNs provide a powerful alternative to traditional
RNNs. Despite their simplicity, they approximate complex time-dependent
functions, enable efficient training without backpropagation, and support
principled network size reduction. Their combination of theoretical elegance and
practical efficiency makes them a valuable tool for time-series analysis and
dynamic system modeling. Future work will focus on extending the procedure, in
particular network size reduction, to RNNs augmented with nonlinear
activation, see also \cite{BKP16}. This would make an even larger class of networks
more efficient.

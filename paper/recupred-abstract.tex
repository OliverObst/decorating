\begin{abstract}
Linear recurrent neural networks (LRNNs) provide a simple yet powerful framework
for modeling time series. Unlike traditional recurrent neural networks, LRNNs
use linear activation functions and can be trained without backpropagation by
solving linear systems. This paper summarizes their theoretical properties,
learning procedure, and ability to approximate arbitrary time-dependent
functions. A key contribution is a method for reducing network size using
eigenvalue analysis, which is nowadays a very important topic, enabling both
efficient learning and architecture optimization. We demonstrate the properties
and advantages of LRNNs by several case studies, among them multiple
superimposed oscillators (MSO), robotic soccer (RoboCup), and stock price
prediction. LRNNs outperform the previous state-of-the-art for the MSO task with
a minimal number of units. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Time-series modeling is essential in many domains such as forecasting, signal
processing, and control systems. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs), especially
LSTMs, are widely used but often require complex training procedures like
backpropagation through time. Research in time-series analysis and hence the
modeling dynamics of complex systems has a long tradition and remains highly
active due to its crucial role in many real-world applications \cite{LBE15},
such as weather forecasts, stock quotations, comprehension of trajectories of
objects and agents, and solving number puzzles \cite{GW13}. Among other
benefits, the analysis of time series enables data compression, i.e., the
compact representation of time series, by a function $f(t)$, and prediction of
future values.

Numerous studies address these topics using RNNs, particularly variants of
networks with long short-term memory (LSTM) \cite{HS97}. Autoregressive
frameworks are also common in current machine-learning applications such as
large language models (LLMs).
Nevertheless, in all these cases, iterative methods like backpropagation are
employed for neural network learning. This is not needed for \emph{linear
recurrent neural networks} (LRNNs), a simplified class of RNNs with linear
activation, which we discuss here. Despite their simplicity, LRNNs (1) can
approximate arbitrary time-dependent functions $f(t)$, (2) can be trained
efficiently using simple linear algebra, and (3)allow direct architecture
reduction via eigenvalue analysis.

\section{Linear Recurrent Neural Networks}

An LRNN consists of neurons connected by network connections.
They are annotated with \emph{weights} which are compiled into the
\emph{transition matrix} $W$ of size $N \times N$. An entry~$w_{ij}$ in row~$i$
and column~$j$ denotes the weight of the edge from neuron~$j$ to neuron~$i$. The
transition matrix has the form
\[
  W = \left[ \begin{array}{cc}
  \multicolumn{2}{c}{W^\mathrm{out}}\\
  W^\mathrm{in} & W^\mathrm{res}
\end{array} \right]
\]
and contains the input, output and reservoir weight matrices. The network
evolves as: $f(t) = W^t s$, where $s$ is the initial state. Key properties of
LRNNs are (1) Linear activation ($g(x)=x$), (2) random input and reservoir
weights, (3) only output weights are learned, and (4) the spectral radius of the
reservoir is normalized to $1$.

Using eigendecomposition $W = VDV^{-1}$ with column eigenvectors $v_1,\dots,v_N$
in $V$ and corresponding eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_N$, the system
evolves as $f(t) = x_1 \lambda_1^t v_1 +\dots+ x_N \lambda_N^t v_N$ with $x =
\big[ x_1 \cdots x_N \big]^\top$ such that $x = V^{-1} \cdot s$. This can be
generalized to Jordan decomposition with Jordan matrix $J$ and to arbitrary
start vectors $y$ with all nonzero entries. Then always a matrx $A$ exists such
that: \[ f(t) = W^t \cdot s = A \cdot J^t \cdot y \]

This shows that network behavior is governed by eigenvalues $\lambda$, in
particular the one with maximal absolute value (defining thze spectral radius.
We have (1) decay for $|\lambda| < 1$, (2) oscillation for $|\lambda| = 1$, and (3)
exponential growth for $|\lambda| > 1$. For large $t$, LRNN dynamics simplify
drastically: Most components vanish, the remaining dynamics lie in at most $2$
dimensions, and the output follows an ellipse trajectory. Thus, complex
high-dimensional systems reduce to simple oscillatory behavior over time.

\section{Learning Procedure and Network Size Reduction}

Training is performed via linear regression instead of backpropagation. Given:
\[
X =
\begin{bmatrix}
S(0) & \cdots & S(n-1) \\
R(0) & \cdots & R(n-1)
\end{bmatrix},
\quad
Y =
\begin{bmatrix}
S(1) & \cdots & S(n)
\end{bmatrix},
\]
where $S(0),\dots,S(n)$ is the possibly multi-dimensional time series to be
learned, we simply solve: $W_{\text{out}} = Y X^{-1}$. This step is efficient
and avoids gradient-based optimization. LRNNs satisfy an approximation theorem:
\emph{Any time-dependent function $f(t)$ can be approximated arbitrarily well
and learned given sufficient reservoir size.} They can represent polynomials,
exponentials, oscillatory signals, solutions to linear differential equation
systems.

A major contribution is reducing network size using eigenvalue analysis. The
method is as follows: After training (1) compute eigenvalues of $W$, (2) rank
the Jordan components of $W$ by importance, (3) remove those with minimal
contribution, and (4)ensure error remains below threshold. This yields
significantly smaller networks with sparse structure, improved generalization of
the time series, and faster computation. Unlike pruning methods, network size
reduction is performed in a \emph{single step}.

\section{Properties, Insights, and Experimental Findings}

The learning procedure even with network size reduction is computationally
efficient: $O(N^3)$. No iterative optimization is required. Therefore, it is
faster than traditional RNN training. LRNN are related to autoregressive models
\cite{Aka69} like current large language models (LLMs), echo state networks
(ESNs) \cite{JH04}, and state-space models \cite{GGR22,OS+23}. However, LRNNs
uniquely combine linear learning, exact approximation, and architecture
reduction.

Experiments demonstrate that LRNNs (1) achieve high accuracy on time-series
prediction, (2) outperform traditional methods on oscillatory tasks, and (3)
learn compact representations (e.g., minimal networks). The following benchmarks
have been considered: multiple superimposed oscillators (MSO) \cite{KLB12},
number puzzles \cite{GW13}, robot soccer simulation \cite{MO+18}, and predicting
stock prices. All experiments are performed with a program written by the
authors in Octave \cite{EB+17}, available at \url{http://github.com/OliverObst/decorating/}.

For instance, the MSO time series is generated by summing up several (pure)
sinusoids. Interestingly, in LRNNs, multiple superimposed oscillators require
only $2K$ neurons, where Each frequency corresponds to one eigenvalue pair.
LRNNs outperform the previous state-of-the-art for the MSO task with this
minimal number of units.

\section{Conclusion}

Linear Recurrent Neural Networks provide a powerful alternative to traditional
RNNs. Despite their simplicity, they approximate complex time-dependent
functions, enable efficient training without backpropagation, and support
principled network size reduction. Their combination of theoretical elegance and
practical efficiency makes them a valuable tool for time-series analysis and
dynamic system modeling. Future work will focus on extend the procedure in
particular on network size reduction to RNNs augmented with nonlinear
activation, see also \cite{BKP16}. This could make a larger class of networks
more efficient.
